The physics of the Roadster wall-flip simulation

Report on sim/wall_flip_sim.py, written 2026-10-17. Every number on this page comes from the code, from a run of it, or from PROJECT.html. Code links go to the file on GitHub, by line. Other pages: videos, 3D replay, development journey.

The problem. On 2026-10-15 a Tesla Roadster drove up a curved wall, rode a 170° overhang upside down, let go, rolled upright in the air and landed on cold-gas thrusters. No source publishes the wall size, the car mass or the thrust. The broadcast shows only a few gauges: pitch, altitude, downforce and G. This page shows how the simulation turns those gauge readings and a few press facts into a complete, checkable physical model.

How to read it. Part 1 derives each equation from first principles. After each derivation, a teal box says the same thing in plain words and names the code line. Part 2 lists every number, with its status. Part 3 compares the result with the HUD and lists what the model cannot show.

Status tags used in Part 2: measured read from the video; published stated by Tesla or the press; derived calculated from other numbers; est. an estimate (an assumption).

Part 1: the theory

0. Setting: clocks, axes, symbols

Clock. All times are seconds on the VideoFromSpace clock (eqOfmtTJD1A). The PCMag clip runs 61.35 s later: PCMag time = sim time + 61.35 s. The header of data/hud_wall_flip.csv still says 61.0 s. That value is old. The gauge readings corrected it to 61.35 s on 2026-10-16.

World axes. \(x\) is horizontal, along the wall's plane. \(x = 0\) is the foot of the curve. The car starts at negative \(x\), climbs toward positive \(x\), and the overhang carries it back over negative \(x\). \(y\) points to the side, and \(z\) points up. The flight model keeps the car in the plane \(y = 0\) (see Known limits).

Body axes. \(x_b\) points forward, \(y_b\) left and \(z_b\) up, from the car's centre of gravity (CG). The rotation matrix \(R\) takes a body vector into world axes: \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{world}} = R\,\mathbf{v}_b\).

Altitude. The HUD ALTITUDE is the CG height above its height when parked. The sim stores the CG at \(z = h + h_{cg}\), with \(h_{cg} = 0.45\) m.

1. Rebuilding the wall from the HUD

No source gives the wall dimensions. During the first, slow climb (0:05.5–0:27 in VideoFromSpace), the HUD shows PITCH \(\theta\) and ALTITUDE \(h\). These two gauges are enough to rebuild the shape of the CG path.

Step 1: the slope relation. Let the car move a short distance \(ds\) along the track at pitch \(\theta\). The CG then rises \(dh = \sin\theta\,ds\) and moves \(dx = \cos\theta\,ds\) sideways. Divide the two to remove \(ds\):

$$\frac{dx}{dh} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(\theta) = \int \frac{dh}{\tan\theta} \approx \sum_k \frac{\Delta h_k}{\tan\bar\theta_k}$$

Here \(\bar\theta_k\) is the mid-point pitch of step \(k\). The code steps \(\theta\) from 0° to 170° in 0.1° steps.

Step 2: the entry curve. Near \(\theta = 0\), \(\tan\theta \to 0\). A 0.1 ft read error in \(h\) then gives a large error in \(x\). So the code drops readings below 20° and uses a circle there. A circle of radius \(R\) that starts flat on the road has \(h = R(1-\cos\theta)\). The least-squares radius minimises \(\sum_i \left[h_i - R(1-\cos\theta_i)\right]^2\). Set the derivative with respect to \(R\) to zero:

$$R = \frac{\sum_i h_i\,(1-\cos\theta_i)}{\sum_i (1-\cos\theta_i)^2}$$

The sum uses the readings between 20° and 80° (27.6° to 76.4°). The result is \(R = 8.656\) m.

Step 3: the blend. Above the circle, a shape-preserving cubic (PCHIP) passes through the readings \(h(\theta)\). Between 20° and 40°, a linear weight \(w = (\theta - 20°)/20°\) moves from the circle to the data: \(h = (1-w)\,R(1-\cos\theta) + w\,h_{\text{PCHIP}}(\theta)\).

Step 4: the ceiling. From the first 170° reading, the pitch gauge stays at 170°. The code makes the rest a straight line at 170°. It must reach the release altitude \(z_{rel}\), which is the lag-corrected HUD altitude at 52.25 s (§10). Its length is

$$L = \frac{z_{rel} - z_{170}}{\sin 170°} + L_{lip}$$

\(L_{lip}\) is the extra ceiling past the release point: 1.5 m in the default hud variant, 0 m in the short variant. Last, the code adds 40 m of flat road in front of the foot and integrates the arc length \(s = \int\sqrt{dx^2 + dz^2}\).

In plain words. If you know how steep the track is and how much the car climbed, you know how far it moved sideways. The code adds up those small sideways steps. Where the track is nearly flat, the method is too sensitive to reading errors, so a circle fills that part. The upside-down ceiling is a straight line.

2. Motion on the wall

On the wall, the model does not predict the motion. It reads the motion from the HUD of the flip run and then computes the forces that this motion needs. This is an inverse (kinematic) model.

Altitude to track position. On the wall, \(dh/ds = \sin\theta > 0\) for \(0 \lt \theta \lt 180°\). So \(h(s)\) increases strictly and has an inverse \(s(h)\). Each lag-corrected HUD altitude gives one track position.

Start position from the G gauge. The car starts from rest at 45.65 s, when G steps from 1.0 to 1.1. The altitude stays below 0.1 ft until 46.3 s. On a flat road, the accelerometer measures the vector difference of the car's acceleration \(a\) and gravity. Its size is \(\sqrt{a^2 + g_0^2}\). So

$$G = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + g_0^2}}{g_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = g_0\sqrt{G^2 - 1} = g_0\sqrt{1.1^2 - 1} = 0.458\,g_0$$

With constant acceleration for 0.65 s (45.65 to 46.3 s), the car travels \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.46\,g_0 \cdot 0.65^2 = 0.95\) m. The code puts the start that far before the point where the altitude first reads 0.1 ft.

Smoothing. The readings \(s_i\) are noisy, and the loads need the second derivative. A smoothing spline \(f(t)\) minimises

$$\sum_i \bigl(s_i - f(t_i)\bigr)^2 + \lambda \int f''(t)^2\,dt, \qquad \lambda = 2\times10^{-3}$$

Speed is \(v = f'(t)\) and acceleration along the track is \(a = f''(t)\). The code samples them every 0.01 s from 44.0 s to the release at 52.25 s. Before 45.65 s it sets \(v = a = 0\).

In plain words. The HUD says how high the car was at each moment. The rebuilt wall says where on the track that height is. A smooth curve through those points gives the speed and the acceleration.

3. Loads on the wall: tyres, suction, G, power

Path frame. At pitch \(\theta\), the unit tangent along the track and the unit normal away from the surface (the car's "up") are

$$\hat{\mathbf t} = (\cos\theta,\ 0,\ \sin\theta), \qquad \hat{\mathbf n} = (-\sin\theta,\ 0,\ \cos\theta)$$

Acceleration on a curved path. Differentiate the velocity \(\mathbf v = v\,\hat{\mathbf t}\). Because \(d\hat{\mathbf t}/ds = \kappa\,\hat{\mathbf n}\) and \(ds/dt = v\):

$$\mathbf a = \dot v\,\hat{\mathbf t} + v\,\frac{d\hat{\mathbf t}}{dt} = a\,\hat{\mathbf t} + v^2\kappa\,\hat{\mathbf n}$$

The second term is the centripetal acceleration. On the concave wall it points away from the surface, into the car's "up" direction.

Forces. Five forces act on the car: tyre traction \(F_t\,\hat{\mathbf t}\); the surface's push \(N\,\hat{\mathbf n}\) with \(N \ge 0\); suction \(-F_s\,\hat{\mathbf n}\), which pulls the car onto the surface; gravity \(m\mathbf g\); and air drag \(-D\,\hat{\mathbf t}\) with \(D = \tfrac12\rho\,(C_dA)_x\,v^2\). Gravity along the two directions is \(m\mathbf g\cdot\hat{\mathbf t} = -mg_0\sin\theta\) and \(m\mathbf g\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = -mg_0\cos\theta\). Newton's second law along each direction gives

$$\text{along } \hat{\mathbf t}:\quad m a = F_t - m g_0\sin\theta - D \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_t = m a + m g_0 \sin\theta + D$$ $$\text{along } \hat{\mathbf n}:\quad m v^2\kappa = N - F_s - m g_0\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = m v^2\kappa + m g_0\cos\theta + F_s$$

Friction limit. The tyres can transmit at most \(\mu N\): \(|F_t| \le \mu N\). Put in \(N\) and solve for the suction:

$$F_s \;\ge\; \frac{|F_t|}{\mu} - m v^2\kappa - m g_0\cos\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_{s,\min} = \max\!\Bigl(0,\ \frac{|F_t|}{\mu} - m v^2\kappa - m g_0\cos\theta\Bigr)$$

On the ceiling, \(\theta = 170°\) and \(\cos\theta = -0.985\). The last term then becomes \(+0.985\,mg_0\): suction must first cancel almost all of the car's weight, and then supply the normal load for traction.

Accelerometer. An accelerometer measures the specific force \(\mathbf a - \mathbf g\), not \(\mathbf a\). At rest it reads \((0, 0, g_0)\), that is 1 G. The model's G gauge is

$$G = \frac{\lvert a\,\hat{\mathbf t} + v^2\kappa\,\hat{\mathbf n} - \mathbf g\rvert}{g_0}$$

Power. The power at the wheels is \(P_w = F_t\,v\).

In plain words. The tyres must push the car up the slope, accelerate it and beat the air. A tyre can only push as hard as it is pressed onto the surface, times the friction coefficient. Upside down, gravity pulls the car away, so the suction fans must press the car on. The model computes the least suction that still lets the tyres grip.

4. Free flight: the rigid body

After release at 52.25 s, the car is a free rigid body with six degrees of freedom (DOF): three of position and three of attitude. The initial state is the last wall state: position \(\mathbf p\), velocity \(v\,\hat{\mathbf t}\), attitude from the path frame \(R = [\hat{\mathbf t},\ \hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf t},\ \hat{\mathbf n}]\), and \(\boldsymbol\omega = 0\).

Translation (Newton). With all non-gravity forces collected in \(\mathbf F_{ng}\) (world axes):

$$m\,\dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf F_{ng} + m\,\mathbf g, \qquad \dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf v, \qquad \mathbf F_{ng} = R\,(\mathbf F_{thrust} + \mathbf F_{drag}) + \mathbf F_{contact}$$

The mass falls as gas leaves: \(m = m_{dry} + m_{gas}(t)\). For a body that ejects mass, the thrust force \(\mathbf F_{thrust}\) already contains the momentum carried away by the gas. So \(m\dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf F\) holds with the current mass and no extra \(\dot m\,\mathbf v\) term.

Rotation (Euler). The angular momentum in body axes is \(I\boldsymbol\omega\). Its rate in an inertial frame, written in rotating body axes, is \(I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} + \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega\). That rate equals the applied moment \(\mathbf M\):

$$I\,\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} = \mathbf M - \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega$$

Attitude (quaternions). A unit quaternion \(q = (w, x, y, z)\) stores the attitude without the singularities of Euler angles. With \(\boldsymbol\omega\) in body axes, its rate is

$$\dot q = \tfrac12\, q \otimes (0, \boldsymbol\omega)$$

where \(\otimes\) is the quaternion product. The rotation matrix follows from \(q\):

$$R(q) = \begin{pmatrix} 1-2(y^2+z^2) & 2(xy - wz) & 2(xz + wy) \\ 2(xy + wz) & 1-2(x^2+z^2) & 2(yz - wx) \\ 2(xz - wy) & 2(yz + wx) & 1-2(x^2+y^2) \end{pmatrix}$$

Integration. The step is \(\Delta t = 1\) ms, semi-implicit Euler: first \(\mathbf v\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega\), then \(\mathbf p\) and \(q\) with the new rates. After each step the code divides \(q\) by its norm, so it stays a unit quaternion. Over 5.75 s that is 5,750 steps.

In plain words. In the air, the car obeys two laws. Force changes its speed. Moment (twist) changes its spin. The spin is stored as a quaternion, a four-number form of attitude that never locks up. The code advances time in 1 ms steps.

5. Forces in flight: nozzles, drag, wheels

Nozzle wrench. Nozzle \(i\) sits at body position \(\mathbf r_i\) and pushes along the unit body direction \(\mathbf d_i\) with thrust \(f_i \ge 0\). Its force is \(f_i\mathbf d_i\) and its moment about the CG is \(\mathbf r_i \times f_i\mathbf d_i\). All ten nozzles together give a six-component wrench, linear in the thrusts:

$$\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf F_{thrust} \\ \mathbf M_{thrust}\end{pmatrix} = B\,\mathbf f, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf d_1 & \cdots & \mathbf d_{10} \\ \mathbf r_1\times\mathbf d_1 & \cdots & \mathbf r_{10}\times\mathbf d_{10} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{6\times10}$$

Drag. The code uses a separate drag area on each body axis \(j\):

$$F_{drag,j} = -\tfrac12\,\rho\,(C_dA)_j\,\lvert v_{b,j}\rvert\,v_{b,j}, \qquad \mathbf v_b = R^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v$$

Wheels. Each of the four wheels is a contact point \(\mathbf r_w = (\pm\tfrac{WB}2,\ \pm\tfrac{TR}2,\ -(h_{cg}+\delta_0))\) in body axes. \(\delta_0 = mg_0/(4k) = 72.0\) mm is the static spring deflection. When the world height \(z_w\) of a wheel point is below 0, the ground pushes up with a spring-damper force, and friction acts on the sliding velocity \(\mathbf v_t\):

$$N = \max\bigl(0,\ -k\,z_w - c\,\dot z_w\bigr), \qquad \mathbf F_{fric} = -\mathbf v_t\,\min\!\Bigl(5000,\ \frac{\mu N}{\lvert\mathbf v_t\rvert}\Bigr)$$

At low sliding speed, friction acts as a 5,000 N·s/m damper. At higher speed, it is Coulomb friction of size \(\mu N\). The damper part removes the jump at \(\mathbf v_t = 0\), which a 1 ms integrator cannot handle. Each wheel force also gives a moment \(R^{\mathsf T}\bigl((R\mathbf r_w)\times\mathbf F\bigr)\) in body axes.

In plain words. Each jet pushes the car and also twists it, more so when it sits far from the centre. One 6×10 table holds both effects for all ten jets. Air drag grows with the square of speed. The wheels are springs with shock absorbers that work only when they touch the ground.

6. The gas: Abel-Noble tank and blowdown

Why not the ideal gas law. The ideal gas law \(Pv = RT\) treats molecules as points. At 690 bar, nitrogen molecules fill a real part of the volume, and the gas is stiffer than the ideal law says. The Abel-Noble equation removes a co-volume \(b\) per kilogram from the space the molecules can move in:

$$P\,(v - b) = R\,T, \qquad v = \frac{V}{m_{gas}}$$

The compressibility factor is \(Z = Pv/(RT) = 1 + bP/(RT)\). With \(b = 8.0\times10^{-4}\) m³/kg, at 690 bar and 293 K, \(Z = 1.635\). The code comment says the co-volume was fitted to \(Z = 1.65\) for N₂ at 690 bar. The value this \(b\) gives at 293 K is 1.635, which is 0.9% lower.

Start state. 250 kg in 0.600 m³ gives \(v_0 = 2.4\times10^{-3}\) m³/kg. Then \(P_0 = RT/(v_0 - b) = 296.8\times293/(1.6\times10^{-3}) = 543.5\) bar. The ideal law would give 362 bar.

Blowdown. Follow the gas that stays in the tank. Its specific volume grows as gas leaves. The first law for one kilogram is \(du = \delta q - P\,dv\). For an Abel-Noble gas, internal energy depends on \(T\) only, because \((\partial u/\partial v)_T = T(\partial P/\partial T)_v - P = TR/(v-b) - P = 0\). So \(du = c_v\,dT\). With no heat flow:

$$c_v\,dT = -\frac{RT}{v - b}\,dv \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dT}{T} = -(\gamma - 1)\,\frac{d(v-b)}{v-b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\,(v-b)^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}$$

This uses \(R/c_v = \gamma - 1\). The tank wall gives heat back to the cold gas, so the real process lies between adiabatic (\(n = \gamma = 1.4\)) and isothermal (\(n = 1\)). The code uses the polytropic form with \(n = 1.25\):

$$T_{k+1} = T_k \left(\frac{v_k - b}{v_{k+1} - b}\right)^{n-1}$$

Each step removes \(\dot m\,\Delta t\) of gas (§7) and then updates \(T\) and \(P\).

In plain words. At 543 bar the gas is so dense that its molecules take up real space, so the pressure is 50% higher than the simple gas law says. When gas leaves, the gas that stays expands and cools, and the pressure falls faster than the mass. Heat from the tank wall slows the cooling. The exponent \(n\) sets how much.

7. The nozzle: thrust and mass flow

Choked flow. At a pressure ratio of hundreds to one, the flow at the nozzle throat reaches the speed of sound. The mass flow then depends only on the tank state and the throat area \(A^*\). For an ideal gas with isentropic flow from the tank to the throat:

$$\dot m = \frac{P\,A^*}{c^*}, \qquad c^* = \frac{\sqrt{R\,T}}{\Gamma}, \qquad \Gamma = \sqrt{\gamma}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}$$

For \(\gamma = 1.4\), \(\Gamma = 0.6847\). The code writes the same quantity as \(c^* = \sqrt{\gamma RT}/\Gamma'\) with \(\Gamma' = \sqrt{\gamma}\,\Gamma = 0.8102\). At 293 K, \(c^* = 430.7\) m/s.

Thrust. The thrust coefficient \(C_f\) collects the exit speed and the exit pressure terms: \(F = C_f\,P\,A^*\). Divide the two relations to remove \(A^*\) and \(P\):

$$\dot m = \frac{F}{C_f\,c^*}, \qquad c_{\text{eff}} = C_f\,c^* = 1.6 \times 430.7 = 689.1\ \text{m/s}$$

Pressure limit. With a fixed throat, \(F \propto P\). So each nozzle's maximum thrust falls with the tank pressure: \(F_{\max,i}(P) = F_{\max,i}^{(0)}\,P/P_0\). The valve can only reduce the thrust below that limit.

Throat sizes (derived). From \(A^* = F_{\max}^{(0)}/(C_f P_0)\): rear 230 mm² (17.1 mm diameter), corner lift 402 mm² (22.6 mm), front 172 mm² (14.8 mm). At full pressure one lift nozzle uses 50.8 kg/s.

In plain words. A cold-gas jet is a hole that the gas leaves at the speed of sound. A bigger hole or a higher pressure gives more thrust and uses gas faster. Each newton of thrust uses gas at 1/689 kg/s. As the tank empties, every jet gets weaker.

8. The flight controller

The demo car was autonomous, so the model flies it with a controller, not a human. The controller runs at 100 Hz (every 10th 1 ms step). It has three references and two feedback laws.

Reference 1: height. \(z_r(t)\) is a smoothing spline (\(\lambda = 10^{-3}\)) through the lag-corrected HUD altitude plus \(h_{cg}\), from 52.20 s on.

Reference 2: horizontal braking. The car leaves the lip with horizontal speed \(v_{x0}\). The controller brakes it to zero by \(T_b = 55.0\) s with a minimum-jerk profile. Let \(u = (t - T_{rel})/(T_b - T_{rel})\), clipped to [0, 1], and

$$S(u) = 10u^3 - 15u^4 + 6u^5, \qquad \dot x_r = v_{x0}\bigl(1 - S(u)\bigr), \qquad x_r = x_0 + v_{x0}(T_b - T_{rel})\int_0^u \bigl(1 - S(u')\bigr)\,du'$$

\(S\) goes from 0 to 1 with zero slope and zero curvature at both ends, so the braking starts and ends without a jerk. Because \(\int_0^1 (1 - S)\,du = \tfrac12\), the braking distance is \(v_{x0}(T_b - T_{rel})/2\).

Reference 3: attitude. The car must half-roll from upside down to upright, between \(t_0 = 52.35\) s and \(t_1 = 53.10\) s, and lose its 10° release pitch by 53.40 s:

$$R_r(t) = R_{up}\;R_y\!\Bigl(\theta_0\bigl(1 - S(u_p)\bigr)\Bigr)\;R_x\!\Bigl(\pi\bigl(1 - S(u_r)\bigr)\Bigr), \qquad u_r = \frac{t - t_0}{t_1 - t_0}, \quad u_p = \frac{t - t_0}{t_1 + 0.3 - t_0}$$

\(R_{up} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -1, 1)\) is upright with the nose toward \(-x\). \(\theta_0 = -10°\) is the release pitch as a rotation about \(y\).

Feedback law 1: position. The wanted acceleration is the reference acceleration plus proportional-derivative (PD) corrections:

$$a_{x} = \ddot x_r + 4(x_r - x) + 4(\dot x_r - \dot x), \qquad a_{y} = 4(0 - y) + 4(0 - \dot y)$$ $$a_{z} = \begin{cases} \ddot z_r + 9(z_r - z) + 4.2(\dot z_r - \dot z), & t \ge T_{burn} = 53.95\ \text{s} \\ -g_0, & t \lt T_{burn} \end{cases}$$

A PD law \(\ddot e + k_d\dot e + k_p e = 0\) has natural frequency \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k_p}\) and damping ratio \(\zeta = k_d/(2\omega_n)\). Horizontal: \(\omega_n = 2\) rad/s, \(\zeta = 1.0\). Vertical: \(\omega_n = 3\) rad/s, \(\zeta = 0.7\). Before the burn, \(a_z = -g_0\) asks for zero vertical non-gravity force: the car falls free. The jets still fire for the roll and the braking, so a small vertical thrust can remain. The required non-gravity force in body axes is

$$\mathbf F_b = R^{\mathsf T}\,m\,(\mathbf a_{des} - \mathbf g)$$

Feedback law 2: attitude. The attitude error is the rotation from the reference to the actual attitude, \(R_e = R_r^{\mathsf T}R\). For a rotation by angle \(\phi\) about unit axis \(\mathbf k\), \(\tfrac12(R_e - R_e^{\mathsf T}) = \sin\phi\,[\mathbf k]_\times\). So the vector

$$\mathbf e = \tfrac12\,\bigl(R_e - R_e^{\mathsf T}\bigr)^{\vee} = \sin\phi\;\mathbf k$$

is a small-angle rotation error (\(^\vee\) takes the three entries of a skew matrix). The reference rate comes from \([\boldsymbol\omega_r]_\times = R_r^{\mathsf T}\dot R_r\), by a central difference over ±0.01 s. The wanted moment is

$$\mathbf M_b = I\bigl(-36\,\mathbf e - 8.4\,(\boldsymbol\omega - \boldsymbol\omega_r)\bigr) + \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega$$

That is \(\omega_n = 6\) rad/s and \(\zeta = 0.7\) on each axis. The last term cancels the gyroscopic term of Euler's equation (§4).

In plain words. The controller has a plan for height (follow the HUD), for forward speed (brake smoothly to a stop by 55.0 s) and for attitude (roll over in 0.75 s). Each 10 ms it compares the car with the plan and asks for a force and a twist that close the gap, like a spring with a shock absorber. Until 53.95 s it lets the car fall freely.

9. The valve allocator

The controller asks for a wrench \(\mathbf w = (\mathbf F_b, \mathbf M_b)\): six numbers. There are ten thrusts, each between 0 and its pressure limit. The allocator finds the thrusts that come closest:

$$\min_{\mathbf f}\ \bigl\lVert W\,(B\mathbf f - \mathbf w)\bigr\rVert^2 + \varepsilon^2\lVert\mathbf f\rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to}\quad 0 \le f_i \le F_{\max,i}(P)$$

\(W = \mathrm{diag}(10^{-3}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3})\) per N and per N·m: one N·m of moment error counts 3 times as much as one N of force error. \(\varepsilon = 10^{-5}\) is a small penalty that picks the lowest total thrust when several answers fit equally. SciPy's lsq_linear solves this bounded-variable least-squares problem.

Valves. A valve does not open at once. The actual thrust follows the command with a first-order lag:

$$\tau\,\dot f_i = f_{cmd,i} - f_i, \qquad \tau = 20\ \text{ms}$$

and is clipped to \([0, F_{\max,i}(P)]\).

Landing cut. When the wheels carry more than half the weight for more than 0.05 s, all commands go to zero and stay there.

In plain words. Jets can only push, and each has a limit. When the wish is more than the jets can give, the allocator picks the best compromise and treats twist errors as more serious than force errors. Valves take about 20 ms to follow.

10. The HUD lag: a free-fall fit

The first full run failed its own check (sensitivity). One cause: the HUD altitude is late. The G gauge drops from 1.0 to 0.4 between 52.2 and 52.3 s, but the altitude keeps rising until 52.6–52.7 s. A car in free fall after release can rise only for \(v_{z0}/g_0\), about 0.14 s.

Model. Before release, the CG climbs the straight 170° ceiling at a constant vertical speed \(v_{z0}\). After release it falls freely. The HUD shows this height \(\ell\) seconds late. With \(\tau = t - \ell - T_{rel}\):

$$\hat h(t) = z_0 + v_{z0}\,\tau - \tfrac12\,g_0\,\max(\tau, 0)^2$$

A parabola alone has only two free parameters, so it cannot separate \(\ell\) from \(z_0\) and \(v_{z0}\). The kink at \(\tau = 0\), where the straight climb changes to the parabola, fixes \(\ell\). The fit uses the 18 HUD readings from 52.2 to 53.95 s, before the burn.

Result. \(\ell = 0.337\) s, \(v_{z0} = 1.329\) m/s, \(z_0 = 20.768\) m, rms residual 1.258 ft. Check: the sim's release state is 8.139 m/s along a 170° ceiling, so \(v_{z0} = 8.139\sin10° = 1.413\) m/s, 6% above the fit. The code uses \(\ell = 0.30\) s. The second stunt's HUD showed about 0.2 s. The record gives no other reason for 0.30 s in place of 0.34 s.

In plain words. A car that lets go of a ceiling can only drift up for a moment before it falls. The HUD altitude kept climbing for 0.4 s, so the gauge is late. Fitting a fall curve to the gauge gives a delay of 0.34 s. The sim shifts all HUD altitudes 0.30 s earlier.
sim/wall_flip_sim.py:51 HUD_LAG; 136–138 applied to all HUD altitudes; fit in out/physics/make_charts.py (the fit code was not in the repository before)

11. Fit metrics and the self-check

The report compares the sim with the HUD on the HUD's own sample times:

  • Altitude RMSE in flight: \(\sqrt{\tfrac1N\sum_i\bigl(h_{sim}(t_i - \ell) - h_{HUD}(t_i)\bigr)^2}\) over the HUD samples with \(T_{rel} + \ell \lt t_i \le 57.2\) s.
  • G RMSE: the same form, without lag, on the wall (46.0 to 52.2 s) and in flight. The G gauge is taken as not lagged.
  • Apex, touchdown, landing tilt, gas, clearance: from the flight record. Touchdown is the first time the wheels carry more than half the weight. Clearance is the smallest distance from a body corner to the wall surface, from 0.3 s after release.

The run stops with an error unless all seven checks pass: apex within 1.5 ft of the HUD; touchdown within 0.4 s; altitude RMSE under 2.5 ft; landing tilt under 10°; gas used between 0 and 250 kg; wall clearance over 0.1 m; no body corner more than 0.05 m below the ground.

The wall

Reading Fig. 2. The speed is about 6.8 m/s in the entry curve and falls to 4.6 m/s on the overhang (49.0–50.5 s). Up to 51.0 s the minimum suction peaks at 3,779 kgf, against 4,700 kgf available: 80% of it. The median from 48.5 to 51.0 s is 1,989 kgf. Wheel power up to 51.0 s peaks at 207 kW, against more than 1,000 kW published. So suction, not motor power, limits the climb.

Why the suction curve is a saw. The swings come from the acceleration \(a = f''(t)\), not from the wall. Example: the HUD altitude jumps from 48.1 to 50.6 ft between 50.2 and 50.3 s. After the 0.30 s lag shift, \(a\) rises to 6.4 m/s² at 49.8 s and falls to −3.2 m/s² at 50.0 s. The suction need follows: 3,777 kgf, then 1,578 kgf.

The ceiling values are noise. On a 170° ceiling, one 0.1 ft altitude step is 0.176 m of track. The sim's peaks there, 5,003 kgf of suction at 51.40 s (speed 10.14 m/s, acceleration 10.62 m/s²), 294 kW and 10.46 m/s, are in this band. They are not physical results. PROJECT.html §4.1 gives the typical overhang need as about 3,300 kgf and the peak power as about 190 kW; those values were sampled every 0.5 s.

Altitude and G

Reading Fig. 3. On the wall, the sim leads the raw HUD by the lag, by design. In flight the sim follows the shifted HUD. The largest gap is in the fall: from 53.1 to 53.4 s (lag-corrected) the sim is 5.3 to 6.3 ft below the shifted HUD.

Reading Fig. 4. On the wall, the sim's G swings by about ±0.5 g around the HUD. The cause is the same acceleration noise as in Fig. 2. In free fall the sim reads 0.0 to 1.4 g; the HUD reads 0.2 to 0.6 g. The sim spikes at 52.6 s and 53.1 s are the lift pairs that start and stop the roll. Both curves rise at about 53.95 s. The sim peaks at once, 3.65 g at 54.04 s; the HUD peaks at 3.3 g at 54.4 s. The spike of 3.78 g at 56.19 s is the wheel impact at touchdown.

Tank and nozzles

Reading Fig. 5. The run uses 76.7% of the gas (191.8 of 250 kg), but the pressure falls by 89% (543.5 to 58.6 bar). At touchdown each nozzle can give only 11.3% of its full-tank thrust.

Reading Fig. 6. Before the burn, the front pair brakes the forward speed and the lift pairs make the roll: first the right pair (FR, RR), then the left pair (FL, RL) to stop it. The outer rear nozzles fire one at a time (rear0 at y = +0.45 m, rear3 at y = −0.45 m). One-sided, they give a yaw moment and some forward push. From 53.95 s the four lift nozzles run at or near the pressure limit until about 55.0 s, then below it for the hover. The burn is limited by pressure, which is why the lift nozzles had to be 35 kN each (sensitivity).

Note that n = 1.1 uses less gas than n = 1.25 but ends at a higher pressure: warmer gas has a higher \(c^*\), so each newton costs less mass (\(\dot m = F/(C_f c^*)\)). The change log recorded 55.75 / 56.19 / 56.27 s for the three exponents before the roll-window change; the table shows the current values.
  • Nozzle sizing. With the first-guess sizes the lift nozzles cannot stop the fall: the car hits the ground at 54.78 s with a 9.81 g wheel impact. The 35 kN lift size is the derived value that gives the 3.3 g burn and the hover.
  • HUD lag. Without a lag model, the controller chased a gauge 0.3 s late and fought the free fall at the top. The first full run touched down at 55.17 s with a peak of 11.0 g (DEVELOPMENT-JOURNEY.html §5.3).
  • Tolerance 2.0 → 2.5 ft. The altitude RMSE limit was 2.0 ft at first. At n = 1.25 the run gave 2.04 ft. The limit was raised to 2.5 ft because the pure free-fall fit to the same HUD data already leaves 1.26 ft: the gauge's 0.1 ft steps and the simple lag model set that floor. The reason is in the code at line 402. With the current code, all three exponents give more than 2.0 ft. A reader should know that the limit moved after a run failed it.

Known limits

  1. Planar flight (y = 0). In shot B and in the replay R1, the real car drifts to +y (screen left). The model keeps it in the plane. A two-camera triangulation of the real path failed its gate (car rms ≤ 5 px in both views; best result 76 px in R1, 50 px in B). Per the owner's rule, the sim was not refitted.
  2. The wall phase is kinematic. The motion on the wall is read from the HUD, not predicted. The loads inherit the gauge noise: the 5,003 kgf suction, the 294 kW power and the 10.46 m/s speed on the ceiling are artefacts. The speed also jumps from 0 to 1.52 m/s at 45.65 s, because the code sets \(v = 0\) before that time while the spline already moves.
  3. Overhang length. Two variants. Shot B wall-edge fits favour the hud length (median rms 3.09 px at +1.5 m against 3.23 px at 0 m). The image at PCMag 113.6 s (= 52.25 s) shows the car at or past the lip, which favours short.
  4. G gauge timing. The model takes the G gauge as not lagged. Shot B shows the lift jets at 53.45 s, 0.5 s before the HUD G rise that sets \(T_{burn}\) = 53.95 s. Either the G gauge also lags, or the jets start before G rises.
  5. Constant inertia and CG. 191.8 kg of gas leaves, 8.2% of the wet mass. The mass in Newton's law falls, but the inertia and the CG position do not change.
  6. Simple nozzle model. \(c^*\) uses the ideal-gas choking relation at the tank temperature; the throat gas at about half the tank pressure is still dense. \(C_f\) is a constant 1.6, although the exit pressure ratio falls from about 540 to 58 during the run. One lumped tank, no pipe losses.
  7. No reference feed-forward for spin rate. The attitude law uses \(\boldsymbol\omega_r\) but not \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_r\).
  8. Altitude zero. The first climb reached 69.1 ft; the flip run let go at 67.8 ft after lag correction. The altitude zero can differ by about 1.3 ft between runs.
  9. Gauge readings. All HUD values were read by eye: 0.1 ft and 0.1 g steps at 10 Hz (flip run) and 2 Hz (first climb).
  10. Estimates. Mass, inertia, \(C_f\), n, the gains and the nozzle positions are estimates. The lift and front nozzle sizes were derived to fit the observed flight. They are consistent with the data, not measured.
