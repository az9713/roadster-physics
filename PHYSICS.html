The physics of the Roadster wall-flip simulation

Report on sim/wall_flip_sim.py, written 2026-10-17. Every number on this page comes from the code, from a run of it, or from PROJECT.html. Code links go to the file on GitHub, by line. Other pages: videos, 3D replay, development journey.

The problem. On 2026-10-15 a Tesla Roadster drove up a curved wall, rode a 170° overhang upside down, let go, rolled upright in the air and landed on cold-gas thrusters. No source publishes the wall size, the car mass or the thrust. The broadcast shows only a few gauges: pitch, altitude, downforce and G. This page shows how the simulation turns those gauge readings and a few press facts into a complete, checkable physical model.

How to read it. Part 1 derives each equation from first principles. After each derivation, a teal box says the same thing in plain words and names the code line. Part 2 lists every number, with its status. Part 3 compares the result with the HUD and lists what the model cannot show. Part 4 analyses the flip itself: the release from the ceiling and the half-roll, with one model for all forces.

Status tags used in Part 2: measured read from the video; published stated by Tesla or the press; derived calculated from other numbers; est. an estimate (an assumption).

0. Setting: clocks, axes, symbols

Clock. All times are seconds on the VideoFromSpace clock (eqOfmtTJD1A). The PCMag clip runs 61.35 s later: PCMag time = sim time + 61.35 s. The header of data/hud_wall_flip.csv still says 61.0 s. That value is old. The gauge readings corrected it to 61.35 s on 2026-10-16.

World axes. \(x\) is horizontal, along the wall's plane. \(x = 0\) is the foot of the curve. The car starts at negative \(x\), climbs toward positive \(x\), and the overhang carries it back over negative \(x\). \(y\) points to the side, and \(z\) points up. The flight model keeps the car in the plane \(y = 0\) (see Known limits).

Body axes. \(x_b\) points forward, \(y_b\) left and \(z_b\) up, from the car's centre of gravity (CG). The rotation matrix \(R\) takes a body vector into world axes: \(\mathbf{v}_{\text{world}} = R\,\mathbf{v}_b\).

Altitude. The HUD ALTITUDE is the CG height above its height when parked. The sim stores the CG at \(z = h + h_{cg}\), with \(h_{cg} = 0.45\) m.

1. Rebuilding the wall from the HUD

No source gives the wall dimensions. During the first, slow climb (0:05.5–0:27 in VideoFromSpace), the HUD shows PITCH \(\theta\) and ALTITUDE \(h\). These two gauges are enough to rebuild the shape of the CG path.

Step 1: the slope relation. Let the car move a short distance \(ds\) along the track at pitch \(\theta\). The CG then rises \(dh = \sin\theta\,ds\) and moves \(dx = \cos\theta\,ds\) sideways. Divide the two to remove \(ds\):

$$\frac{dx}{dh} = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(\theta) = \int \frac{dh}{\tan\theta} \approx \sum_k \frac{\Delta h_k}{\tan\bar\theta_k}$$

Here \(\bar\theta_k\) is the mid-point pitch of step \(k\). The code steps \(\theta\) from 0° to 170° in 0.1° steps.

Step 2: the entry curve. Near \(\theta = 0\), \(\tan\theta \to 0\). A 0.1 ft read error in \(h\) then gives a large error in \(x\). So the code drops readings below 20° and uses a circle there. A circle of radius \(R\) that starts flat on the road has \(h = R(1-\cos\theta)\). The least-squares radius minimises \(\sum_i \left[h_i - R(1-\cos\theta_i)\right]^2\). Set the derivative with respect to \(R\) to zero:

$$R = \frac{\sum_i h_i\,(1-\cos\theta_i)}{\sum_i (1-\cos\theta_i)^2}$$

The sum uses the readings between 20° and 80° (27.6° to 76.4°). The result is \(R = 8.656\) m.

Step 3: the blend. Above the circle, a shape-preserving cubic (PCHIP) passes through the readings \(h(\theta)\). Between 20° and 40°, a linear weight \(w = (\theta - 20°)/20°\) moves from the circle to the data: \(h = (1-w)\,R(1-\cos\theta) + w\,h_{\text{PCHIP}}(\theta)\).

Step 4: the ceiling. From the first 170° reading, the pitch gauge stays at 170°. The code makes the rest a straight line at 170°. It must reach the release altitude \(z_{rel}\), which is the lag-corrected HUD altitude at 52.25 s (§10). Its length is

$$L = \frac{z_{rel} - z_{170}}{\sin 170°} + L_{lip}$$

\(L_{lip}\) is the extra ceiling past the release point: 1.5 m in the default hud variant, 0 m in the short variant. Last, the code adds 40 m of flat road in front of the foot and integrates the arc length \(s = \int\sqrt{dx^2 + dz^2}\).

In plain words. If you know how steep the track is and how much the car climbed, you know how far it moved sideways. The code adds up those small sideways steps. Where the track is nearly flat, the method is too sensitive to reading errors, so a circle fills that part. The upside-down ceiling is a straight line.

2. Motion on the wall

On the wall, the model does not predict the motion. It reads the motion from the HUD of the flip run and then computes the forces that this motion needs. This is an inverse (kinematic) model.

Altitude to track position. On the wall, \(dh/ds = \sin\theta > 0\) for \(0 \lt \theta \lt 180°\). So \(h(s)\) increases strictly and has an inverse \(s(h)\). Each lag-corrected HUD altitude gives one track position.

Start position from the G gauge. The car starts from rest at 45.65 s, when G steps from 1.0 to 1.1. The altitude stays below 0.1 ft until 46.3 s. On a flat road, the accelerometer measures the vector difference of the car's acceleration \(a\) and gravity. Its size is \(\sqrt{a^2 + g_0^2}\). So

$$G = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + g_0^2}}{g_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = g_0\sqrt{G^2 - 1} = g_0\sqrt{1.1^2 - 1} = 0.458\,g_0$$

With constant acceleration for 0.65 s (45.65 to 46.3 s), the car travels \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.46\,g_0 \cdot 0.65^2 = 0.95\) m. The code puts the start that far before the point where the altitude first reads 0.1 ft.

Smoothing. The readings \(s_i\) are noisy, and the loads need the second derivative. A smoothing spline \(f(t)\) minimises

$$\sum_i \bigl(s_i - f(t_i)\bigr)^2 + \lambda \int f''(t)^2\,dt, \qquad \lambda = 2\times10^{-3}$$

Speed is \(v = f'(t)\) and acceleration along the track is \(a = f''(t)\). The code samples them every 0.01 s from 44.0 s to the release at 52.25 s. Before 45.65 s it sets \(v = a = 0\).

In plain words. The HUD says how high the car was at each moment. The rebuilt wall says where on the track that height is. A smooth curve through those points gives the speed and the acceleration.

3. Loads on the wall: tyres, suction, G, power

Path frame. At pitch \(\theta\), the unit tangent along the track and the unit normal away from the surface (the car's "up") are

$$\hat{\mathbf t} = (\cos\theta,\ 0,\ \sin\theta), \qquad \hat{\mathbf n} = (-\sin\theta,\ 0,\ \cos\theta)$$

Acceleration on a curved path. Differentiate the velocity \(\mathbf v = v\,\hat{\mathbf t}\). Because \(d\hat{\mathbf t}/ds = \kappa\,\hat{\mathbf n}\) and \(ds/dt = v\):

$$\mathbf a = \dot v\,\hat{\mathbf t} + v\,\frac{d\hat{\mathbf t}}{dt} = a\,\hat{\mathbf t} + v^2\kappa\,\hat{\mathbf n}$$

The second term is the centripetal acceleration. On the concave wall it points away from the surface, into the car's "up" direction.

Forces. Five forces act on the car: tyre traction \(F_t\,\hat{\mathbf t}\); the surface's push \(N\,\hat{\mathbf n}\) with \(N \ge 0\); suction \(-F_s\,\hat{\mathbf n}\), which pulls the car onto the surface; gravity \(m\mathbf g\); and air drag \(-D\,\hat{\mathbf t}\) with \(D = \tfrac12\rho\,(C_dA)_x\,v^2\). Gravity along the two directions is \(m\mathbf g\cdot\hat{\mathbf t} = -mg_0\sin\theta\) and \(m\mathbf g\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = -mg_0\cos\theta\). Newton's second law along each direction gives

$$\text{along } \hat{\mathbf t}:\quad m a = F_t - m g_0\sin\theta - D \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_t = m a + m g_0 \sin\theta + D$$ $$\text{along } \hat{\mathbf n}:\quad m v^2\kappa = N - F_s - m g_0\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = m v^2\kappa + m g_0\cos\theta + F_s$$

Friction limit. The tyres can transmit at most \(\mu N\): \(|F_t| \le \mu N\). Put in \(N\) and solve for the suction:

$$F_s \;\ge\; \frac{|F_t|}{\mu} - m v^2\kappa - m g_0\cos\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad F_{s,\min} = \max\!\Bigl(0,\ \frac{|F_t|}{\mu} - m v^2\kappa - m g_0\cos\theta\Bigr)$$

On the ceiling, \(\theta = 170°\) and \(\cos\theta = -0.985\). The last term then becomes \(+0.985\,mg_0\): suction must first cancel almost all of the car's weight, and then supply the normal load for traction.

Accelerometer. An accelerometer measures the specific force \(\mathbf a - \mathbf g\), not \(\mathbf a\). At rest it reads \((0, 0, g_0)\), that is 1 G. The model's G gauge is

$$G = \frac{\lvert a\,\hat{\mathbf t} + v^2\kappa\,\hat{\mathbf n} - \mathbf g\rvert}{g_0}$$

Power. The power at the wheels is \(P_w = F_t\,v\).

In plain words. The tyres must push the car up the slope, accelerate it and beat the air. A tyre can only push as hard as it is pressed onto the surface, times the friction coefficient. Upside down, gravity pulls the car away, so the suction fans must press the car on. The model computes the least suction that still lets the tyres grip.

4. Free flight: the rigid body

After release at 52.25 s, the car is a free rigid body with six degrees of freedom (DOF): three of position and three of attitude. The initial state is the last wall state: position \(\mathbf p\), velocity \(v\,\hat{\mathbf t}\), attitude from the path frame \(R = [\hat{\mathbf t},\ \hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf t},\ \hat{\mathbf n}]\), and \(\boldsymbol\omega = 0\).

Translation (Newton). With all non-gravity forces collected in \(\mathbf F_{ng}\) (world axes):

$$m\,\dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf F_{ng} + m\,\mathbf g, \qquad \dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf v, \qquad \mathbf F_{ng} = R\,(\mathbf F_{thrust} + \mathbf F_{drag}) + \mathbf F_{contact}$$

The mass falls as gas leaves: \(m = m_{dry} + m_{gas}(t)\). For a body that ejects mass, the thrust force \(\mathbf F_{thrust}\) already contains the momentum carried away by the gas. So \(m\dot{\mathbf v} = \mathbf F\) holds with the current mass and no extra \(\dot m\,\mathbf v\) term.

Rotation (Euler). The angular momentum in body axes is \(I\boldsymbol\omega\). Its rate in an inertial frame, written in rotating body axes, is \(I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} + \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega\). That rate equals the applied moment \(\mathbf M\):

$$I\,\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} = \mathbf M - \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega$$

Attitude (quaternions). A unit quaternion \(q = (w, x, y, z)\) stores the attitude without the singularities of Euler angles. With \(\boldsymbol\omega\) in body axes, its rate is

$$\dot q = \tfrac12\, q \otimes (0, \boldsymbol\omega)$$

where \(\otimes\) is the quaternion product. The rotation matrix follows from \(q\):

$$R(q) = \begin{pmatrix} 1-2(y^2+z^2) & 2(xy - wz) & 2(xz + wy) \\ 2(xy + wz) & 1-2(x^2+z^2) & 2(yz - wx) \\ 2(xz - wy) & 2(yz + wx) & 1-2(x^2+y^2) \end{pmatrix}$$

Integration. The step is \(\Delta t = 1\) ms, semi-implicit Euler: first \(\mathbf v\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega\), then \(\mathbf p\) and \(q\) with the new rates. After each step the code divides \(q\) by its norm, so it stays a unit quaternion. Over 5.75 s that is 5,750 steps.

In plain words. In the air, the car obeys two laws. Force changes its speed. Moment (twist) changes its spin. The spin is stored as a quaternion, a four-number form of attitude that never locks up. The code advances time in 1 ms steps.

5. Forces in flight: nozzles, drag, wheels

Nozzle wrench. Nozzle \(i\) sits at body position \(\mathbf r_i\) and pushes along the unit body direction \(\mathbf d_i\) with thrust \(f_i \ge 0\). Its force is \(f_i\mathbf d_i\) and its moment about the CG is \(\mathbf r_i \times f_i\mathbf d_i\). All ten nozzles together give a six-component wrench, linear in the thrusts:

$$\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf F_{thrust} \\ \mathbf M_{thrust}\end{pmatrix} = B\,\mathbf f, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf d_1 & \cdots & \mathbf d_{10} \\ \mathbf r_1\times\mathbf d_1 & \cdots & \mathbf r_{10}\times\mathbf d_{10} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{6\times10}$$

Drag. The code uses a separate drag area on each body axis \(j\):

$$F_{drag,j} = -\tfrac12\,\rho\,(C_dA)_j\,\lvert v_{b,j}\rvert\,v_{b,j}, \qquad \mathbf v_b = R^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v$$

Wheels. Each of the four wheels is a contact point \(\mathbf r_w = (\pm\tfrac{WB}2,\ \pm\tfrac{TR}2,\ -(h_{cg}+\delta_0))\) in body axes. \(\delta_0 = mg_0/(4k) = 72.0\) mm is the static spring deflection. When the world height \(z_w\) of a wheel point is below 0, the ground pushes up with a spring-damper force, and friction acts on the sliding velocity \(\mathbf v_t\):

$$N = \max\bigl(0,\ -k\,z_w - c\,\dot z_w\bigr), \qquad \mathbf F_{fric} = -\mathbf v_t\,\min\!\Bigl(5000,\ \frac{\mu N}{\lvert\mathbf v_t\rvert}\Bigr)$$

At low sliding speed, friction acts as a 5,000 N·s/m damper. At higher speed, it is Coulomb friction of size \(\mu N\). The damper part removes the jump at \(\mathbf v_t = 0\), which a 1 ms integrator cannot handle. Each wheel force also gives a moment \(R^{\mathsf T}\bigl((R\mathbf r_w)\times\mathbf F\bigr)\) in body axes.

In plain words. Each jet pushes the car and also twists it, more so when it sits far from the centre. One 6×10 table holds both effects for all ten jets. Air drag grows with the square of speed. The wheels are springs with shock absorbers that work only when they touch the ground.

6. The gas: Abel-Noble tank and blowdown

Why not the ideal gas law. The ideal gas law \(Pv = RT\) treats molecules as points. At 690 bar, nitrogen molecules fill a real part of the volume, and the gas is stiffer than the ideal law says. The Abel-Noble equation removes a co-volume \(b\) per kilogram from the space the molecules can move in:

$$P\,(v - b) = R\,T, \qquad v = \frac{V}{m_{gas}}$$

The compressibility factor is \(Z = Pv/(RT) = 1 + bP/(RT)\). With \(b = 8.0\times10^{-4}\) m³/kg, at 690 bar and 293 K, \(Z = 1.635\). The code comment says the co-volume was fitted to \(Z = 1.65\) for N₂ at 690 bar. The value this \(b\) gives at 293 K is 1.635, which is 0.9% lower.

Start state. 250 kg in 0.600 m³ gives \(v_0 = 2.4\times10^{-3}\) m³/kg. Then \(P_0 = RT/(v_0 - b) = 296.8\times293/(1.6\times10^{-3}) = 543.5\) bar. The ideal law would give 362 bar.

Blowdown. Follow the gas that stays in the tank. Its specific volume grows as gas leaves. The first law for one kilogram is \(du = \delta q - P\,dv\). For an Abel-Noble gas, internal energy depends on \(T\) only, because \((\partial u/\partial v)_T = T(\partial P/\partial T)_v - P = TR/(v-b) - P = 0\). So \(du = c_v\,dT\). With no heat flow:

$$c_v\,dT = -\frac{RT}{v - b}\,dv \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dT}{T} = -(\gamma - 1)\,\frac{d(v-b)}{v-b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\,(v-b)^{\gamma-1} = \text{const}$$

This uses \(R/c_v = \gamma - 1\). The tank wall gives heat back to the cold gas, so the real process lies between adiabatic (\(n = \gamma = 1.4\)) and isothermal (\(n = 1\)). The code uses the polytropic form with \(n = 1.25\):

$$T_{k+1} = T_k \left(\frac{v_k - b}{v_{k+1} - b}\right)^{n-1}$$

Each step removes \(\dot m\,\Delta t\) of gas (§7) and then updates \(T\) and \(P\).

In plain words. At 543 bar the gas is so dense that its molecules take up real space, so the pressure is 50% higher than the simple gas law says. When gas leaves, the gas that stays expands and cools, and the pressure falls faster than the mass. Heat from the tank wall slows the cooling. The exponent \(n\) sets how much.

7. The nozzle: thrust and mass flow

Choked flow. At a pressure ratio of hundreds to one, the flow at the nozzle throat reaches the speed of sound. The mass flow then depends only on the tank state and the throat area \(A^*\). For an ideal gas with isentropic flow from the tank to the throat:

$$\dot m = \frac{P\,A^*}{c^*}, \qquad c^* = \frac{\sqrt{R\,T}}{\Gamma}, \qquad \Gamma = \sqrt{\gamma}\left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}$$

For \(\gamma = 1.4\), \(\Gamma = 0.6847\). The code writes the same quantity as \(c^* = \sqrt{\gamma RT}/\Gamma'\) with \(\Gamma' = \sqrt{\gamma}\,\Gamma = 0.8102\). At 293 K, \(c^* = 430.7\) m/s.

Thrust. The thrust coefficient \(C_f\) collects the exit speed and the exit pressure terms: \(F = C_f\,P\,A^*\). Divide the two relations to remove \(A^*\) and \(P\):

$$\dot m = \frac{F}{C_f\,c^*}, \qquad c_{\text{eff}} = C_f\,c^* = 1.6 \times 430.7 = 689.1\ \text{m/s}$$

Pressure limit. With a fixed throat, \(F \propto P\). So each nozzle's maximum thrust falls with the tank pressure: \(F_{\max,i}(P) = F_{\max,i}^{(0)}\,P/P_0\). The valve can only reduce the thrust below that limit.

Throat sizes (derived). From \(A^* = F_{\max}^{(0)}/(C_f P_0)\): rear 230 mm² (17.1 mm diameter), corner lift 402 mm² (22.6 mm), front 172 mm² (14.8 mm). At full pressure one lift nozzle uses 50.8 kg/s.

In plain words. A cold-gas jet is a hole that the gas leaves at the speed of sound. A bigger hole or a higher pressure gives more thrust and uses gas faster. Each newton of thrust uses gas at 1/689 kg/s. As the tank empties, every jet gets weaker.

8. The flight controller

The demo car was autonomous, so the model flies it with a controller, not a human. The controller runs at 100 Hz (every 10th 1 ms step). It has three references and two feedback laws.

Reference 1: height. \(z_r(t)\) is a smoothing spline (\(\lambda = 10^{-3}\)) through the lag-corrected HUD altitude plus \(h_{cg}\), from 52.20 s on.

Reference 2: horizontal braking. The car leaves the lip with horizontal speed \(v_{x0}\). The controller brakes it to zero by \(T_b = 55.0\) s with a minimum-jerk profile. Let \(u = (t - T_{rel})/(T_b - T_{rel})\), clipped to [0, 1], and

$$S(u) = 10u^3 - 15u^4 + 6u^5, \qquad \dot x_r = v_{x0}\bigl(1 - S(u)\bigr), \qquad x_r = x_0 + v_{x0}(T_b - T_{rel})\int_0^u \bigl(1 - S(u')\bigr)\,du'$$

\(S\) goes from 0 to 1 with zero slope and zero curvature at both ends, so the braking starts and ends without a jerk. Because \(\int_0^1 (1 - S)\,du = \tfrac12\), the braking distance is \(v_{x0}(T_b - T_{rel})/2\).

Reference 3: attitude. The car must half-roll from upside down to upright, between \(t_0 = 52.35\) s and \(t_1 = 53.10\) s, and lose its 10° release pitch by 53.40 s:

$$R_r(t) = R_{up}\;R_y\!\Bigl(\theta_0\bigl(1 - S(u_p)\bigr)\Bigr)\;R_x\!\Bigl(\pi\bigl(1 - S(u_r)\bigr)\Bigr), \qquad u_r = \frac{t - t_0}{t_1 - t_0}, \quad u_p = \frac{t - t_0}{t_1 + 0.3 - t_0}$$

\(R_{up} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -1, 1)\) is upright with the nose toward \(-x\). \(\theta_0 = -10°\) is the release pitch as a rotation about \(y\).

Feedback law 1: position. The wanted acceleration is the reference acceleration plus proportional-derivative (PD) corrections:

$$a_{x} = \ddot x_r + 4(x_r - x) + 4(\dot x_r - \dot x), \qquad a_{y} = 4(0 - y) + 4(0 - \dot y)$$ $$a_{z} = \begin{cases} \ddot z_r + 9(z_r - z) + 4.2(\dot z_r - \dot z), & t \ge T_{burn} = 53.95\ \text{s} \\ -g_0, & t \lt T_{burn} \end{cases}$$

A PD law \(\ddot e + k_d\dot e + k_p e = 0\) has natural frequency \(\omega_n = \sqrt{k_p}\) and damping ratio \(\zeta = k_d/(2\omega_n)\). Horizontal: \(\omega_n = 2\) rad/s, \(\zeta = 1.0\). Vertical: \(\omega_n = 3\) rad/s, \(\zeta = 0.7\). Before the burn, \(a_z = -g_0\) asks for zero vertical non-gravity force: the car falls free. The jets still fire for the roll and the braking, so a small vertical thrust can remain. The required non-gravity force in body axes is

$$\mathbf F_b = R^{\mathsf T}\,m\,(\mathbf a_{des} - \mathbf g)$$

Feedback law 2: attitude. The attitude error is the rotation from the reference to the actual attitude, \(R_e = R_r^{\mathsf T}R\). For a rotation by angle \(\phi\) about unit axis \(\mathbf k\), \(\tfrac12(R_e - R_e^{\mathsf T}) = \sin\phi\,[\mathbf k]_\times\). So the vector

$$\mathbf e = \tfrac12\,\bigl(R_e - R_e^{\mathsf T}\bigr)^{\vee} = \sin\phi\;\mathbf k$$

is a small-angle rotation error (\(^\vee\) takes the three entries of a skew matrix). The reference rate comes from \([\boldsymbol\omega_r]_\times = R_r^{\mathsf T}\dot R_r\), by a central difference over ±0.01 s. The wanted moment is

$$\mathbf M_b = I\bigl(-36\,\mathbf e - 8.4\,(\boldsymbol\omega - \boldsymbol\omega_r)\bigr) + \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega$$

That is \(\omega_n = 6\) rad/s and \(\zeta = 0.7\) on each axis. The last term cancels the gyroscopic term of Euler's equation (§4).

In plain words. The controller has a plan for height (follow the HUD), for forward speed (brake smoothly to a stop by 55.0 s) and for attitude (roll over in 0.75 s). Each 10 ms it compares the car with the plan and asks for a force and a twist that close the gap, like a spring with a shock absorber. Until 53.95 s it lets the car fall freely.

9. The valve allocator

The controller asks for a wrench \(\mathbf w = (\mathbf F_b, \mathbf M_b)\): six numbers. There are ten thrusts, each between 0 and its pressure limit. The allocator finds the thrusts that come closest:

$$\min_{\mathbf f}\ \bigl\lVert W\,(B\mathbf f - \mathbf w)\bigr\rVert^2 + \varepsilon^2\lVert\mathbf f\rVert^2 \quad \text{subject to}\quad 0 \le f_i \le F_{\max,i}(P)$$

\(W = \mathrm{diag}(10^{-3}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3})\) per N and per N·m: one N·m of moment error counts 3 times as much as one N of force error. \(\varepsilon = 10^{-5}\) is a small penalty that picks the lowest total thrust when several answers fit equally. SciPy's lsq_linear solves this bounded-variable least-squares problem.

Valves. A valve does not open at once. The actual thrust follows the command with a first-order lag:

$$\tau\,\dot f_i = f_{cmd,i} - f_i, \qquad \tau = 20\ \text{ms}$$

and is clipped to \([0, F_{\max,i}(P)]\).

Landing cut. When the wheels carry more than half the weight for more than 0.05 s, all commands go to zero and stay there.

In plain words. Jets can only push, and each has a limit. When the wish is more than the jets can give, the allocator picks the best compromise and treats twist errors as more serious than force errors. Valves take about 20 ms to follow.

10. The HUD lag: a free-fall fit

The first full run failed its own check (sensitivity). One cause: the HUD altitude is late. The G gauge drops from 1.0 to 0.4 between 52.2 and 52.3 s, but the altitude keeps rising until 52.6–52.7 s. A car in free fall after release can rise only for \(v_{z0}/g_0\), about 0.14 s.

Model. Before release, the CG climbs the straight 170° ceiling at a constant vertical speed \(v_{z0}\). After release it falls freely. The HUD shows this height \(\ell\) seconds late. With \(\tau = t - \ell - T_{rel}\):

$$\hat h(t) = z_0 + v_{z0}\,\tau - \tfrac12\,g_0\,\max(\tau, 0)^2$$

A parabola alone has only two free parameters, so it cannot separate \(\ell\) from \(z_0\) and \(v_{z0}\). The kink at \(\tau = 0\), where the straight climb changes to the parabola, fixes \(\ell\). The fit uses the 18 HUD readings from 52.2 to 53.95 s, before the burn.

Result. \(\ell = 0.337\) s, \(v_{z0} = 1.329\) m/s, \(z_0 = 20.768\) m, rms residual 1.258 ft. Check: the sim's release state is 8.139 m/s along a 170° ceiling, so \(v_{z0} = 8.139\sin10° = 1.413\) m/s, 6% above the fit. The code uses \(\ell = 0.30\) s. The second stunt's HUD showed about 0.2 s. The record gives no other reason for 0.30 s in place of 0.34 s.

In plain words. A car that lets go of a ceiling can only drift up for a moment before it falls. The HUD altitude kept climbing for 0.4 s, so the gauge is late. Fitting a fall curve to the gauge gives a delay of 0.34 s. The sim shifts all HUD altitudes 0.30 s earlier.

11. Fit metrics and the self-check

The report compares the sim with the HUD on the HUD's own sample times:

  • Altitude RMSE in flight: \(\sqrt{\tfrac1N\sum_i\bigl(h_{sim}(t_i - \ell) - h_{HUD}(t_i)\bigr)^2}\) over the HUD samples with \(T_{rel} + \ell \lt t_i \le 57.2\) s.

The run stops with an error unless all seven checks pass: apex within 1.5 ft of the HUD; touchdown within 0.4 s; altitude RMSE under 2.5 ft; landing tilt under 10°; gas used between 0 and 250 kg; wall clearance over 0.1 m; no body corner more than 0.05 m below the ground.

Part 4: the flip

The problem. For about one second the car does three things that no other phase of the stunt combines. It hangs upside down under a 170° ceiling, held there only by suction. It lets go. Then, in free fall, it turns 180° about its own long axis and arrives upright before the landing burn. The car has no wings and no rudder. Ten cold-gas jets that can only push, gravity and the air are the only things that can turn it. This part asks which of those forces does the work, how large each one is, and what the turn costs.

Window. From 51.31 s (the track reaches 170°, the car is inverted and the suction holds it) to 53.95 s (the landing burn starts). Before the window, the car climbs the curved overhang. After it, the burn of §8 stops the fall.

Method. Part 4 is an analysis of the existing sim, not a change to it. sim/wall_flip_sim.py and its self-check are unchanged. Section 4.3 writes one equation set for the whole window, with every force and moment. The terms that the sim leaves out are then evaluated along the sim's own trajectory, so that each one has a size. The script out/physics/flip.py prints every number in this part.

4.1 Before: riding the ceiling

The force balance upside down. On the straight ceiling, the curvature is \(\kappa = 0\). The normal equation of §3 then has no centripetal term:

$$N = F_s + m g_0\cos\theta = F_s - 0.985\,m g_0 \qquad (\theta = 170°)$$

The surface can only push, so \(N \ge 0\). The suction must first carry 98.5% of the weight, 2,314 kgf, before it presses the tyres on at all. The tyres must then carry the along-track load \(F_t = m a + m g_0\sin\theta + D\).

Worked case: the least suction at constant speed. Take \(a = 0\) at the release speed \(v = 8.139\) m/s. The drag is \(D = \tfrac12\rho\,(C_dA)_x v^2 = 27.8\) N, so \(F_t = 4{,}030\) N. With \(\mu = 1.1\):

$$F_{s,\min} = \frac{|F_t|}{\mu} - m g_0\cos\theta = 26{,}359\ \text{N} = 2{,}688\ \text{kgf}$$

That is 57% of the 4,700 kgf that the HUD showed as the peak downforce. The ceiling values of the sim's \(F_{s,\min}\) in Fig. 2 are larger, but they come from gauge noise in \(a\) (Part 3).

No spin at the start. On the curved overhang the car pitches at \(\dot\theta = v\kappa\); its largest value from 50.0 s to 51.31 s is 38.2°/s in the sim. On the straight ceiling \(\kappa = 0\), so \(\dot\theta = 0\). A car that lets go of a straight ceiling therefore starts its flight with \(\boldsymbol\omega = 0\), which is the sim's initial condition (§4). Any turn after the release must come from a moment applied in the air.

The accelerometer cannot see the suction. While the car is attached at \(a = 0\), the non-gravity force is \((N - F_s)\,\hat{\mathbf n} + (F_t - D)\,\hat{\mathbf t} = m g_0\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf n} + m g_0\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf t}\), whose size is \(m g_0\). The G gauge reads 1.0 for any suction above the minimum. It drops only when the car leaves the surface and the contact force goes to zero. So the G step from 1.0 to 0.4 between 52.2 and 52.3 s dates the separation, not the moment the suction was switched off. The HUD reads 1.0–1.1 g from 51.3 to 52.2 s, in agreement.

In plain words. Upside down, the suction has to hold up almost the whole car before the tyres can grip. At a steady 8 m/s it needs about 2,700 kgf, a bit more than half of what the fans gave. The ceiling is straight, so the car is not turning when it lets go. The G gauge stays at 1 G while the car hangs on, whatever the suction does, and falls only when the car comes off.

4.2 Letting go: separation from the ceiling

The condition. When the suction falls below \(0.985\,mg_0 + |F_t|/\mu\), the tyres slip; when it falls below \(0.985\,mg_0\), the push \(N\) reaches zero and the car leaves. The sim models this as an instant switch at \(T_{rel} = 52.25\) s.

The gap. After release, gravity has a part \(g_0|\cos\theta| = 0.985\,g_0\) along the normal away from the ceiling and a part \(g_0\sin\theta = 0.174\,g_0\) against the motion along it. With no thrust, the gap below the plane of the ceiling grows as

$$h_{gap}(\tau) = h_0 + \tfrac12\,(0.985\,g_0)\,\tau^2, \qquad \tau = t - T_{rel}$$

where \(h_0 = 0.120\) m is the distance from the body to the surface at release in the sim's box model. The free-fall part reaches 0.5 m after \(\tau = 0.322\) s. The sim's own gap, which includes the roll, is 0.170 m at 52.35 s, 0.312 m at 52.45 s and 0.485 m at 52.55 s.

How much ceiling is left. In the hud variant the wall ends 1.5 m past the nominal release point. The smoothing spline of §2 puts the sim's car 0.392 m past that point at 52.25 s, so only 1.108 m of ceiling is ahead of the CG. Solving \(1.108 = v\tau - \tfrac12(0.174\,g_0)\tau^2\) gives \(\tau = 0.138\) s: the CG passes the lip at 52.388 s, when the free-fall gap is 0.092 m. The rear bumper, 2.35 m behind the CG, passes it at 52.696 s. In the short variant the lip is at the release point: the CG passes it at 52.25 s and the rear bumper at 52.548 s. Fig. 8 shows the hud case.

A check the self-check skips. The wall-clearance check of §11 starts 0.3 s after the release, because the car starts on the surface. Over the skipped 0.3 s, the smallest body-to-ceiling gap in the sim is 0.120 m, at 52.25 s itself. The gap only grows after that, also during the start of the roll. No contact is hidden in the skipped window.

In plain words. When the suction stops, the car falls away from the ceiling almost as fast as it would fall from a roof. It travels about 1.1 m more under the ceiling (in the longer-ceiling version), then it is past the edge. By then the gap is under 10 cm, so the roll must not start much earlier.

4.3 One model for the whole window

The phases before and after the release use one rigid body with one set of equations. Only the active forces change. Let \(c(t) = 1\) while the car touches the ceiling and \(c(t) = 0\) after it leaves. In world axes:

$$m\,\dot{\mathbf v} = m\mathbf g + R\bigl(B_F\,\mathbf f + \mathbf F_{aero} + \Delta\mathbf F_{jet}\bigr) + c(t)\bigl[(N - F_s)\,\hat{\mathbf n} + (F_t - D)\,\hat{\mathbf t}\bigr]$$

and in body axes:

$$I\,\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} + \boldsymbol\omega\times I\boldsymbol\omega = B_M\,\mathbf f + \mathbf M_{aero} + \Delta\mathbf M_{jet} + c(t)\,\mathbf M_{contact}$$

\(B_F\) and \(B_M\) are the force and moment rows of the nozzle matrix \(B\) (§5). The contact obeys the complementarity conditions

$$h_{gap} \ge 0, \qquad N \ge 0, \qquad h_{gap}\,N = 0$$

that is, either the car touches and the surface pushes, or there is a gap and no push. The aerodynamic terms are

$$\mathbf F_{aero} = -\tfrac12\rho\,(C_dA)\,\lvert\mathbf v_b\rvert\,\mathbf v_b, \qquad \mathbf M_{aero} = \mathbf r_{cp}\times\mathbf F_{aero} + M_{damp}(p, V)\,\mathbf e_x$$

with \(\mathbf r_{cp}\) the offset of the centre of pressure from the CG and \(M_{damp}\) the roll damping (§4.8). \(\Delta\mathbf F_{jet}\), \(\Delta\mathbf M_{jet}\) are the forces that the jets induce on the body through the surrounding air: entrainment in free air and suction near a surface (§4.9). 4.4 The half-roll as a rigid-body problem

The roll equation. Take the \(x_b\) row of Euler's equation:

$$I_{xx}\,\dot p = M_x - (I_{zz} - I_{yy})\,q\,r$$

The sim's peak \(|q| = 0.321\) rad/s and \(|r| = 0.279\) rad/s give a gyroscopic term of at most 15.3 N·m. That is 0.059% of the peak jet moment, so in practice \(I_{xx}\dot p = M_x\). With \(\varphi\) as defined in §4.0 and small pitch, \(\dot\varphi \approx p\).

Proposition 1 (peak rate and acceleration of the half-roll). Let the roll follow \(\varphi(t) = \pi\,\bigl(1 - S(u)\bigr)\) with \(u = (t - t_0)/T\) and the minimum-jerk curve \(S(u) = 10u^3 - 15u^4 + 6u^5\) of §8. Then the largest roll rate and roll acceleration are

$$\lvert\dot\varphi\rvert_{\max} = \frac{15\pi}{8\,T} = 7.854\ \text{rad/s}, \qquad \lvert\ddot\varphi\rvert_{\max} = \frac{10\sqrt3}{3}\,\frac{\pi}{T^2} = 32.25\ \text{rad/s}^2 \qquad (T = 0.75\ \text{s})$$

Proof. \(\dot\varphi = -\pi S'(u)/T\) and \(\ddot\varphi = -\pi S''(u)/T^2\), with \(S'(u) = 30u^2(1-u)^2\), \(S''(u) = 60u - 180u^2 + 120u^3\) and \(S'''(u) = 60 - 360u + 360u^2\). \(S'\) is largest where \(S'' = 0\) inside \((0,1)\): \(u = \tfrac12\), \(S'(\tfrac12) = 30/16 = 15/8 = 1.875\). \(|S''|\) is largest where \(S''' = 0\): \(u = (3 \mp \sqrt3)/6\), where \(|S''| = 10\sqrt3/3 = 5.7735\). Multiply by \(\pi/T\) and \(\pi/T^2\). ∎

The moment and the thrust it needs. The moment at the peak is \(I_{xx}\,\ddot\varphi_{\max} = 650 \times 32.25 = 20.96\) kN·m. The lift nozzles push along \(z_b\) at \(y = \pm d\), so a nozzle of thrust \(f\) gives a roll moment \(\pm d f\). Two nozzles on one side give the moment with \(f = M/(2d) = 12.33\) kN each, which is 35% of their 35 kN rating. The angular impulse to reach the peak rate is \(I_{xx}\dot\varphi_{\max} = 5{,}105\) N·m·s for each half of the roll.

The estimate that sets it. \(I_{xx} = 650\) kg·m² is an estimate (Part 2). The thrust need scales with it:

4.5 Jets that only push: the roll throws the car sideways

A wing can make a pure moment. A set of push-only jets cannot always do so. For the lift nozzles the cost is unavoidable.

Proposition 2 (a roll moment needs a net push). Let the four lift nozzles have thrusts \(f_i \ge 0\) along \(z_b\) at \(y = \pm d\). Their roll moment \(M_x\) and their total force \(F_z\) along \(z_b\) satisfy \(F_z \ge |M_x|/d\).

Proof. \(M_x = d\,(f_{FL} + f_{RL} - f_{FR} - f_{RR})\). By the triangle inequality and \(f_i \ge 0\), \(|M_x| \le d\,(f_{FL} + f_{RL} + f_{FR} + f_{RR}) = d\,F_z\). Equality holds when one side is off. ∎

The other jets do not escape this: the rear and front nozzles push in the \(x_b y_b\) plane, and their roll arm is at most 0.034 m per newton (the front pair), which gives at most 438 N·m in the sim, 1.7% of the peak jet moment.

Where the push goes. In world axes, the body \(z_b\) axis at roll angle \(\varphi\) (pitch neglected) is \((0,\ \sin\varphi,\ \cos\varphi)\). The sim confirms it: at \(\varphi = 90°\) (52.80 s) the world \(y\) part of \(z_b\) is +0.999. During the whole roll \(0 \lt \varphi \lt \pi\), so \(\sin\varphi \gt 0\): every pulse, the one that starts the spin and the one that stops it, pushes toward \(+y\).

Proposition 3 (the sideways impulse of the half-roll). With the least jet forces of Proposition 2 and the roll of Proposition 1, the impulse of the lift jets is

$$J_y = \frac{I_{xx}}{d}\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\lvert\ddot\varphi\rvert\sin\varphi\,dt = 4{,}038\ \text{N·s}, \qquad J_z = \frac{I_{xx}}{d}\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\lvert\ddot\varphi\rvert\cos\varphi\,dt = 0$$

Proof. By Proposition 2, the least body-\(z\) force is \(I_{xx}|\ddot\varphi|/d\), and its world direction is \((0, \sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)\). The minimum-jerk curve is symmetric about the midpoint: \(S(1-u) = 1 - S(u)\), so \(\varphi(t_0 + t_1 - t) = \pi - \varphi(t)\) and \(|\ddot\varphi|\) takes the same value at \(t\) and \(t_0 + t_1 - t\). Under this reflection \(\cos\varphi\) changes sign and \(\sin\varphi\) does not. So the \(z\) integral is zero and the \(y\) integral is twice its first half. The value 4,038 N·s is the numerical integral. ∎

The size. \(J_y/m = 1.72\) m/s. Without a sideways correction, the car would drift 0.64 m by the end of the roll and 2.11 m by the burn. The vertical parts cancel: the first pulse pushes down (body \(z_b\) points down while inverted) and the second pushes up. Their magnitudes add up to 5.11 m/s of velocity change, all spent to turn the car.

What the sim does with it. The sim's controller holds \(y = 0\) (§8). Fig. 11 (bottom) shows the result: the sim's \(v_y\) still reaches +0.987 m/s at 52.96 s, the controller then pulls it back to −0.521 m/s at 53.34 s, and the largest offset is 0.333 m. The open-loop line rises to 1.72 m/s and stays there.

Link to the video. In shot B and in the replay R1, the real car drifts to \(+y\) (screen left) (Known limits 1). The model gives a mechanism with that sign, if the real car rolled the same way as the sim (roof toward \(+y\) at mid-roll). The 10 fps roll frames are too small to show the roof side, so the roll direction of the real car is not measured. The triangulation that would measure the drift failed its gate.

In plain words. The jets can push but cannot pull. To start the spin, the jets on one side fire; to stop it, the jets on the other side fire. Both pushes have a sideways part in the same direction, because the car is on its side between them. So a jet roll also throws the car sideways, about 1.7 m/s here. The real car did drift sideways. The sim's autopilot fights the drift and keeps the car on its line.

4.6 What the sim's roll did

Reading Fig. 9. The reference meets the video: it passes 90° at 52.725 s and is upright at 53.10 s. The sim does not follow the reference closely. It passes 90° at 52.800 s, 0.075 s after the reference and 0.10 s after the end of the video's "on its side" interval. It passes upright at 53.00 s, overshoots to −34.3° at 53.18 s, and is still at −3.0° at 53.60 s. At 53.10 s, when the video shows the car upright, the sim is at −28.9°. Its peak roll rate is 9.02 rad/s against 7.854 rad/s, and its peak roll acceleration 39.8 rad/s² against 32.25 rad/s².

The cause. The attitude law of §8 has a natural frequency of 6 rad/s and damping 0.7, and it uses the reference rate \(\boldsymbol\omega_r\) but not the reference acceleration \(\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_r\) (Known limits 7). The moment therefore rises only after an attitude error has built up, so the roll starts late, and the error then drives an overshoot. The valve lag of 20 ms adds to the delay. A feed-forward moment \(I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}_r\) is the standard fix. It is not applied here, because this part does not change the sim. The landing tilt is still 0.0°.

Reading Fig. 10. The jet moment peaks at 26.0 kN·m and \(I_{xx}\dot p\) at 25.9 kN·m; the two curves lie on each other. This checks that the recorded attitude obeys the roll equation with no hidden term. The first pulse (FR + RR) peaks at 24.9 kN at 52.640 s (FR 13.4 kN, RR 11.5 kN, limit 33.0 kN), at \(\varphi = 157°\). The second pulse (FL + RL) peaks at 30.1 kN at 53.060 s (FL 15.5 kN, RL 14.6 kN), at \(\varphi = -21°\), that is, after the car has already rolled past upright. The lower panel shows that the jet moment is 2 to 3 orders of magnitude larger than every other term during the roll.

In plain words. The planned roll matches the video. The sim's autopilot follows it about 0.1 s late, turns too far (34.3° past upright) and needs half a second to settle. The cause is a simple feedback law with no "look-ahead" term. The jets supply all of the twist; nothing else comes close.

4.7 The G gauge during the roll

Fig. 11 (middle) shows a clear difference. During the roll, 52.35–53.10 s, the HUD shows 0.2–0.4 g; the sim shows 0.00–1.40 g. The roll pulses cause the sim's two humps.

Proposition 4 (a bound on the roll inertia from the gauge). Suppose the gauge shows the unfiltered size of the specific force, and the roll moment comes from the lift jets. A rest-to-rest turn by \(\pi\) in time \(T\) needs a peak \(|\ddot\varphi| \ge 4\pi/T^2\). If the gauge never exceeds \(G_{\max}\) during the roll, then

$$I_{xx} \le \frac{G_{\max}\,g_0\,m\,d\,T^2}{4\pi}$$

Proof. The rate starts and ends at zero, so it rises to some peak \(\dot\varphi_{pk}\) and falls back. Let \(\alpha = \max|\ddot\varphi|\). The rate cannot exceed \(\alpha t\) from the start or \(\alpha(T - t)\) from the end, so the angle turned is at most \(\int_0^T \alpha\min(t, T-t)\,dt = \alpha T^2/4\). It must equal \(\pi\), so \(\alpha \ge 4\pi/T^2\). By Proposition 2 the lift-jet force along \(z_b\) is then at least \(I_{xx}\alpha/d\) at that instant. The other jets push in the \(x_b y_b\) plane, which is perpendicular to \(z_b\), so they cannot reduce the size of the specific force. Hence \(G_{\max}\,g_0 \ge I_{xx}\alpha/(m d)\). Drag along \(z_b\) is at most 327 N in the roll, 1.4% of the weight; the bound neglects it. ∎

The numbers. With \(G_{\max} = 0.4\), \(T = 0.75\) s: \(I_{xx} \le 351\) kg·m². If the roll took \(T = 0.85\) s (one more video frame), \(I_{xx} \le 451\) kg·m². For the minimum-jerk shape itself, the bound is 243 kg·m². The estimate is 650 kg·m², and a uniform box of the car's size and mass gives 1,042 kg·m². So at least one of these is true:

  1. The G gauge does not show the unfiltered specific force. It may be filtered or averaged. The HUD altitude is already known to lag by 0.30 s (§10).
  2. The roll inertia is at most about half of the estimate (351 / 650 = 54%).
  3. Part of the roll comes from something other than the lift jets, for example a moment applied before the car leaves the ceiling.

An example of the third case: if one side's suction let go first, the still-attached wheel line would act as a hinge. Gravity's normal part about that line, \(0.985\,mg_0 \times d = 19.3\) kN·m, would then start the roll with \(8.22\) rad/s² (inertia about the hinge 2,348 kg·m²) and no jet force. The record has no evidence for or against this.

In plain words. If the jets alone turned the car, the G gauge should have jumped to about 1 G twice during the roll. It stayed at 0.2–0.4 G. Either the gauge smooths its value, or the car is much easier to turn than estimated, or something else helped start the roll.

4.8 Aerodynamics along the sim's path

Dynamic pressure. The airspeed is 8.13 m/s at release and 14.10 m/s at the burn. So \(q = \tfrac12\rho V^2\) goes from 39.6 Pa to 119.2 Pa. For scale, the car's weight spread over its 9.40 m² plan area is 2,452 Pa. The air is weak during the flip.

Drag (in the sim). The largest drag force in the window is 929 N, 4.03% of the weight, at the burn, when the car falls fastest. During the roll the drag is at most 327 N.

Roll damping (not in the sim). A rolling car moves its floor, roof and sides through the air. Two simple models bound the moment.

  1. Cross-flow. A strip of the plan at lateral position \(y\) moves at \(p\,y\) normal to itself and feels a pressure \(\tfrac12\rho\,C_{D}\,(py)^2\). The moment of all strips is
$$M_{cf} = \tfrac12\rho\,L\,p\lvert p\rvert\left[C_{Dz}\frac{(W/2)^4}{2} + C_{Dy}\frac{(H/2)^4}{2}\right]$$

since \(\int_{-a}^{a} y^2|y|\,dy = a^4/2\). The drag coefficients come from the sim's own drag areas: \(C_{Dz} = 8.0/(L W) = 0.851\) and \(C_{Dy} = 2.0/(L H) = 0.370\). This gives \(M_{cf} = 1.257\,p|p|\) N·m.

  1. Slender wing. In the forward flow \(V\), a strip at \(y\) sees an angle of attack \(py/V\). Slender-wing theory gives a lift slope \(C_{L\alpha} = \pi A/2\) with aspect ratio \(A = W/L = 0.426\), so \(C_{L\alpha} = 0.668\). The moment is
$$M_{sw} = \tfrac12\rho V^2 C_{L\alpha}\,L\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{p y}{V}\,y\,dy = \tfrac12\rho\,C_{L\alpha}\,L\,\frac{2}{3}\Bigl(\frac W2\Bigr)^3\,V p = 1.257\,V p\ \text{N·m}$$

On the sim's path the peaks are 102 N·m and 119 N·m: 0.39% and 0.46% of the peak jet moment. If no controller corrected it, the sum of the two would change the roll angle by 2.51° over the roll. The two constants are equal by coincidence of the numbers, not by a physical rule.

Centre-of-pressure offset (not in the sim). The sim puts the aerodynamic force at the CG. Each 0.1 m of offset adds a moment of \(0.1\,|\mathbf F_{aero}|\): at most 93 N·m in the window, 0.36% of the jet peak, or 0.032 rad/s² in pitch.

Jet-induced lift loss in free air (not in the sim). A jet drags the nearby air with it, which lowers the pressure on the body around the nozzle. Kuhn, Bellavia, Corsiglia and Wardwell (NASA TM 102268, 1991, p. 5) give, for open air,

$$\frac{\Delta L_\infty}{T} = K_\infty\sqrt{\frac{S}{A_j}}\left(\frac{\Sigma\pi d}{d_e}\right)^{1.58}\mathrm{NPR}^{-0.5}, \qquad K_\infty = -0.00010$$

with \(S\) the plan area, \(A_j\) the total jet area, \(\Sigma\pi d\) the total jet perimeter, \(d_e\) the diameter of one jet with the total area, and NPR the nozzle pressure ratio. For one active pair (two jets), \(\Sigma\pi d/d_e = \sqrt2\,\pi\). The jet diameter is not known. The throat is 22.6 mm; ideal expansion from NPR 536 to 1 bar gives an exit Mach number of 5.01, an area ratio of 25.2 and an exit of 114 mm. The result is \(\Delta L_\infty/T = -0.10\%\) to \(-0.55\%\), at most 131 N on a 24 kN pair. The suckdown data in the 1985 survey were taken at NPR 1.45–2.4 (NASA TM 86825, Figs. 3 and 9); here the NPR is 435–536, 180 to 370 times larger. Take the value as an order of magnitude only.

In plain words. At 8–14 m/s the air pushes on the car with less than 5% of its weight. The air also resists the spin, but with about 0.5% of what the jets apply. The sim leaves those out, and they would change the roll by about 2.5° if nobody corrected it. The jet-induced suction in open air is smaller still.

4.9 The jets and the ceiling

Where the exhaust goes. The lift nozzles push along \(+z_b\), so their exhaust leaves along \(-z_b\). Upside down, \(-z_b\) points up, at the ceiling. The sim's first lift thrust above 0.5 kN comes at 52.400 s, when the CG is at \(x = -11.66\) m, already 0.2 m past the hud lip (\(x = -11.46\) m). The rear nozzles are 1.4 m behind the CG, so the rear right nozzle (RR) is still under the ceiling.

The geometry in the sim. In the hud variant, the axis of the RR exhaust meets the ceiling from 52.40 to 52.56 s, at 0.36–0.87 m from the nozzle, with up to 9.5 kN of thrust. In the short variant it meets the ceiling only from 52.40 to 52.42 s, with at most 1.2 kN. No other lift nozzle points at the ceiling while it fires.

The physics. A jet that hits a nearby surface spreads along it as a wall jet. The wall jet drags air out of the gap between the body and the surface, and the low pressure in the gap pulls the body toward the surface ("suckdown"). Here the surface is the ceiling, so the pull is upward, against the RR thrust, which pushes the car down and starts the roll. The pull therefore reduces both the push and the roll moment of RR. For a single jet in hover over a large ground board, Wyatt's correlation (Kuhn and Eshleman, NASA TM 86825, 1985, Fig. 2) is

$$\frac{\Delta L}{T} = -0.012\left(\frac{h}{D - d}\right)^{-2.3}$$

where \(h\) is the height of the plan surface above the ground, \(D\) the effective diameter of the plan, and \(d\) the jet diameter. Its plotted data span \(h/(D - d) \approx 0.17\) to 1.5. For the car, \(D \approx 3.46\) m (circle of equal area) and \(d = 0.114\) m, so the range is \(h = 0.57\) to 5.02 m. At \(h = 0.57\) m, the correlation gives a loss of 70% of the jet thrust. The real gap during the event is 0.12 to 0.49 m, below the fitted range, where the formula gives more than 100%. The correlation cannot give a size here, for three reasons:

  1. It is for a hovering body over a still surface. Here the car slides along the ceiling at 8 m/s and falls away from it.
  2. It is for a large ground board. The ceiling is 3.3 m wide, less than the plan diameter of 3.46 m, and it ends at the lip, 1.22 m from the RR nozzle at 52.40 s.
  3. The gap is below the fitted range, and the NPR (435–536) is 180 to 370 times the 1.45–2.4 of the survey's data.

A scale for the effect. At the bottom of its range the correlation gives 70% of the thrust, and at \(h = 0.49\) m it gives 100%. So take full cancellation of the RR thrust as the scale: while its axis meets the ceiling, the RR impulse is 769 N·s, or 0.327 m/s of speed. Its roll part is 654 N·m·s, 12.8% of the angular impulse of one half of the roll. This is the only aerodynamic term in the window that could matter at the 10% level, and it is the only one that depends on the overhang variant: the hud ceiling exposes it for 0.16 s, the short ceiling for 0.02 s.

In plain words. When the first jets fire, the car is upside down, so they blow up at the last part of the ceiling. A jet blowing at a nearby wall sucks the body toward that wall. Here that would pull against the jet that starts the roll. Standard formulas for this effect do not fit a car sliding past a narrow ceiling edge. If it cancelled that jet completely, it would take away about one eighth of the start-up spin, and only with the longer ceiling.

4.10 After: into the burn, and what the model captures

Into the burn. By 53.40 s the release pitch of −10° is gone in the reference (the sim shows +0.87°). At 53.90 s the sim is upright within 2°. From release to the burn, the jets use 58.6 kg of gas. By thrust impulse, the front pair (braking and yaw) takes 48%, the lift nozzles (the roll and its correction) 42%, and the rear nozzles 10%. At 53.95 s the four lift nozzles open together for the burn of §8.

What the model captures.

  • The inverted force balance on the ceiling and the reason the G gauge marks the separation (§4.1).
  • The fall away from the ceiling and the time left under it, for both overhang variants (§4.2).
  • The rigid-body roll: the moment, the thrust it needs, and its dependence on \(I_{xx}\) (§4.4).
  • The coupling that push-only jets force between turning and translating, with a sideways push toward \(+y\) (§4.5).
  • The size of every aerodynamic term on the sim's own path (§4.8).

What it misses, and where it is discussed.

  • The sim's roll lags its reference by about 0.1 s and overshoots by 34.3° (§4.6). A feed-forward term would repair it; the sim is unchanged by request.
  • The G gauge during the roll does not agree with a jet-only roll at the estimated inertia (§4.7).
  • The release is an instant switch; the suction decay and any one-sided release are not modelled (§4.2, §4.7).
  • The flight is planar (\(y = 0\) is enforced by the controller), so the sideways push is removed, not predicted (§4.5).
  • The jet-ceiling interaction has a scale, not a value (§4.9).
